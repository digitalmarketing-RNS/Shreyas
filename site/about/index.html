How I got here

I came into SEO in the early 2010s, when the Indian market treated it as arithmetic. You bought directory links by the hundred, spun articles, stuffed keywords into footers, and it worked. Then Panda and Penguin landed and most of that industry evaporated more or less overnight.

A lot of people left. I stayed, partly because the collapse was the interesting bit. When the shortcuts stopped working, the actual question surfaced: why does Google prefer one page over another? That question is still the job. The tools change, the algorithm changes, the answer stays roughly the same — the page that most obviously, most completely satisfies the person searching tends to win, and everything else is removing the obstacles that stop Google seeing it.

Since then I've worked across manufacturing, education, legal and compliance services, industrial equipment, real estate, healthcare and consumer services — 66+ brands, mostly Indian, mostly businesses where a single enquiry is worth real money. I work under my own name as an SEO freelancer in Bangalore, and I do the work myself.

What ten years actually buys you

Experience in SEO isn't a stack of certificates. It's pattern recognition, and it comes from having been wrong enough times to recognise the shapes. Concretely, it means:

  • I've been through a dozen core updates with live client sites on the line. I know what a genuine algorithmic hit looks like versus a seasonal dip versus a tracking error — and the difference matters enormously, because the wrong diagnosis costs three months.
  • I've seen what survives. Rankings built on real depth hold. Rankings built on volume tactics come off in the next update. When I refuse to do something, it's usually because I've watched it fail with someone else's money.
  • I know the Indian SERP specifically. Which directories still pass value here, how Justdial and IndiaMART interact with organic results, how Bangalore's language mixing shows up in query data, why "near me" behaves differently in Whitefield than in Malleswaram. That knowledge isn't in any international SEO course.
  • I can tell a real problem from a distracting one. Most audit tools produce 400 issues. Maybe six of them are why you aren't ranking. Sorting those six from the noise is most of the value of hiring someone experienced.

How I work

Everything stays in your accounts. Your Search Console, your Analytics, your Google Business Profile, your hosting. I get access, not ownership. If we part ways you keep everything, including the dashboards. This matters more than people realise — losing history because an agency owned the property is one of the most common and most expensive problems I'm asked to fix.

I report over your own data. Monthly, on a call, looking at your Search Console and GA4 rather than a PDF I built. Branded reports exist to make the work look larger than it was. Reading the raw data together takes the same half hour and you learn something.

I tell you when something didn't work. Some months are flat. Some tests fail. A consultant who reports only good news is either lucky or editing, and after twenty years the industry has plenty of the second kind. Honest reporting is the reason the average client stays over two years without a contract.

I'll turn down work I can't do well. If your site needs a rebuild before SEO makes any sense, or your market is genuinely too contested for the budget, or you need four channels staffed from next month — I'll say so on the first call. It costs me a retainer and saves you six months.

What I won't do

  • Guarantee rankings. Google's own guidance warns against SEOs who do. Nobody controls the algorithm.
  • Buy links from networks. It works until it doesn't, and the recovery costs more than the gains.
  • Publish AI-generated filler at volume. It ranks briefly and then Google's helpful-content systems find it.
  • Hide behind vanity metrics. Impressions are not enquiries. If traffic is up and your phone isn't ringing, we have a problem, and I'll be the one to raise it.
  • Lock you into a contract. If the work is good you'll stay. If it isn't, a contract just delays an honest conversation.

The bit most consultant bios leave out.

I'm one person. That means limited capacity — I take on a small number of clients at a time, and if I'm full I'll say so. It also means no continuity cover if I'm ill for a fortnight. That's a real trade-off against an agency, and you should weigh it. What you get in exchange is that the person who diagnosed your site is the person doing the work, every month, for years.
